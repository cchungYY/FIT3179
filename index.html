Fishing the Australian ocean

Australia manages one of the largest ocean territories in the world. Where do our fishing fleets work, what does Australian seafood add up to, and are the fish stocks holding up? Scroll down to follow the story in three steps.

1 · Where

Where do we fish?

Every year, ABARES maps where boats in Commonwealth-managed fisheries catch fish. Darker patches mean more fish caught from each square kilometre of ocean. Paler patches mean less.

Start with the slider

Drag the year slider from 2010 to 2024. In the early 2010s the darkest patches are mostly in the Great Australian Bight and off south-eastern Australia. From 2016 they are mostly off the east coast.

How to read it: colour shows relative fishing intensity (low, medium or high). Hover over a patch to see the actual cut-offs. They change every year: “low” means under 60 kg of catch per km² in 2010 but under 300 kg in 2024, so compare where the dark patches are, not how many there are.

Empty ocean does not always mean no fishing. ABARES only maps places where five or more vessels fished.

Put the four maps side by side and the pattern is easier to see: fishing is not spread evenly. It clusters in a few regions, and the darkest patches move from the south to the east coast.

Growing or shrinking?

Across all regions, the area of ocean where fishing was recorded fell from about 4.7 million km² in 2010 to 3.5 million km² in 2024, a drop of 26%. The Torres Strait and Coral Sea fell the most (down 53%). The south-east was the only region that grew (up 12%).

How to read it: each line links 2010 (grey) to 2024 (teal). If the teal dot is to the left of the grey dot, the region has shrunk. A smaller area can partly mean fewer boats, because a square is only mapped when enough vessels fished there. The regions are our own grouping of 1° squares by position.

Long-standing fishing grounds

Fishing was recorded in 710 one-degree squares at least once between 2010 and 2024. Of these, 200 (28%) were fished in all 15 years and 152 (21%) in only one. The Great Australian Bight is the most settled region: 28 of its 57 squares were fished every year. Northern waters are the most changeable: 53 of 164 squares were fished in just one year.

How to read it: each hexagon groups about five 1° squares. Darker means the fished squares inside it were fished in more years. Pale hexagons are waters fished only now and then. The regions are the same ones used in the chart above.

2 · What

What comes out of the water?

Together, fishing and fish farming were worth about $3.7 billion in 2024–25 (preliminary figures). Yet 63% of the seafood Australians eat is imported. So what does Australia produce at home, and where does it come from?

Farming has overtaken fishing

Wild-catch fisheries earned more than fish farms in every year from 2010–11 to 2018–19. From 2019–20, farmed seafood moved ahead. In 2024–25 it was worth about $2.1 billion, or 56% of the total, compared with $1.6 billion from wild catch.

How to read it: the height of each coloured band is that sector's value in a given year. Stacked together, the top edge is the combined value.

One small fish dominates by weight

Australian sardines make up 28% of everything caught in the wild by weight: about 50,000 of 180,000 tonnes. But sardines are cheap. That whole catch was worth $36 million, while about 11,000 tonnes of rock lobster was worth $623 million.

How to read it: bars are tonnes, sorted from largest to smallest. The line adds each group to a running share of all wild-caught tonnes. The ten groups shown make up 58% of the total; the rest is “other” fish and many smaller groups.

The same catch, a different price

Australians have caught between about 150,000 and 185,000 tonnes of wild seafood every year since 2010–11. The dollar value moved much more. Between 2018–19 and 2020–21 the tonnes caught fell by 3%, yet their value fell by 18%.

How to read it: moving right means more fish, moving up means more dollars. Follow the line from 2010–11 to 2024–25.

Where it comes from

Tasmania (78,000 tonnes) and South Australia (75,000 tonnes) produced far more than any other state. They get there in opposite ways. 92% of Tasmania's production is farmed, mostly salmon and trout. About three-quarters of South Australia's is wild-caught.

How to read it: bigger circles mean more tonnes. Teal circles are mostly wild-caught and orange circles are mostly farmed. Fisheries managed by the Commonwealth are not part of the state totals.

Fish are not all worth the same

A tonne from the Torres Strait fishery, where rock lobster makes most of the value, was worth about $25,000. A tonne from the Northern Prawn Fishery was worth about $15,500. A tonne from the south-east trawl fishery was worth about $4,100, roughly one-sixth of the Torres Strait price.

How to read it: further right means more tonnes, higher up means more dollars. A fishery that sits close to a steeper dashed line earns more for each kilogram. South-east trawl is the trawl sector of the Southern and Eastern Scalefish and Shark Fishery.

3 · How well

Are fish stocks healthy?

Scientists check whether each fish stock is being fished at a level it can sustain. The national report, SAFS, rates stocks as sustainable, recovering, depleting or depleted. A sustainable stock can replenish itself. A depleting stock is fished too heavily and may fall too low. A depleted stock is already low and is still fished too heavily. A recovering stock is low but is being rebuilt.

The 2024 snapshot

Of every 100 stocks that scientists could rate, 81 were sustainable and 12 were depleted. Another 111 stocks are left out: 39 are caught in very small amounts and 72 do not have enough data to rate.

How to read it: one square is one in every hundred rated stocks. Source: Status of Australian Fish Stocks (SAFS) 2024.

A snapshot only tells part of the story. For the fish stocks managed by the Commonwealth, ABARES also publishes a history. It asks two questions of each stock: is the stock big enough (not overfished), and is it being fished too hard (not overfishing)? A stock is healthy when the answer to both is yes. The healthy band grew from 10 stocks in 2004 to 65 in 2023, but the overfished band barely changed (14 stocks in 2004, 14 in 2023) while the uncertain band shrank from 35 to 21. In 2024, 46 of the 102 stocks were listed as not assessed.

Then and now

Strip away the years in between and compare 2008, the first year when nine in ten stocks were assessed, with 2024. Of the stocks that were assessed, the healthy share rose from 43% to 66%. The share of overfished stocks fell from 14% to 7%.

How to read it: each coloured line is one status. The labels show the three largest statuses; hover over any dot for the rest. Only 56 stocks were assessed in 2024, against 90 in 2008, so the 2024 shares rest on fewer stocks.

Not every fishery is equal

In 2023, every assessed stock in the Northern Prawn Fishery (6 stocks) and the Small Pelagic Fishery (7 stocks) was healthy. In the Coral Sea Fishery only 3 of 8 stocks (38%) were.

How to read it: darker green means a larger share of that fishery's assessed stocks are healthy. Blank squares mean no stock in that fishery was assessed that year. In 2024 several large fisheries, including the south-east trawl fishery, were not assessed. Fisheries with few stocks swing a lot when a single stock changes.

What to take away

Australia's seafood story is shifting from the sea to the farm, and from weight to value: farmed seafood now earns more than wild catch, and what a catch is worth depends far more on the species than on the tonnes. Commonwealth fish stocks are in better shape than two decades ago, with about two-thirds of assessed stocks healthy, but the overfished group has not shrunk and more than a third of stocks are still uncertain or flagged. Meanwhile the ocean area where fishing was recorded has shrunk by a quarter since 2010, which may partly reflect fewer boats.
